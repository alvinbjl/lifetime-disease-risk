1 Goal

Given a cross-sectional dataset (a slice in time) with incidence rate (disease count/total population). we want to estimate the “lifetime risk”, a cumulative probability for a person to get this disease throughout their lifespan.

With this measure, the motivation to supplement health professionals with evidence on managing disease X, answering questions such as:

  1. Burden of cancer on healthcare system? How many patients expected to be treated per year?
  2. What age is the best for cancer screening?
  3. Does different cancer type have diffent optimal age for screening?
  4. Is the cancer screening effective at earlier cancer diagnostic/reach enough people?
  5. Does cancer happen in younger age than before?

2 Issues

Important 1: Misconception

With cross sectional dataset of 100 people and 10 new cases of cancer. Probability of newborn getting cancer is 10%..

2.1 Issue 1: People May Develop Cancer Later

Population of study might still get cancer in later ages. Some are still children.

Counter: Filter population of study to be people who died.

  1. We would ignore everyone who is alive, lose a lot of information.
  2. Oudated data since those who died might developed cancer decades ago.

Counter: To not waste information

In theory we could, just use the data up to certain age. For example if partient A is current 50 years old and had cancer previously. This would increase the rate of cancer by 50 year old. However, the difficutly is the assumption that we have patients record throughout their whole life (Longitudinal data), which usually might not be complete or is expensive/difficutly to get for millions/billions of population.

2.2 Issue 2: Death Is a Competing Risk

Death is a “competing risk” (i.e. Newborn may die before getting cancer.)

Caution

An extreme example: If all infant vaccines are suddenly ineffective, most of our newborn in our test case likely die before reaching old enough age for (10% rate) cancer to be observed.

3 Methods

3.1 Accounting for Age Groups

Tip 1 motivates us to make a rate by age groups. For the toy example

Age Population New Cancer Cases Death Rate Deaths
1 100 60 20 0.6 0.2
2 100 50 10 0.5 0.1
ImportantThe 100 people of age 1 and age 2 in our cross-sectional dataset represent different groups of people.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\text{cancer}) &\neq \mathbb{P}(\text{cancer } Y_1) + \mathbb{P}(\text{cancer } Y_2) \\ &= 0.6 + 0.5 \\ &= 1.1 \end{aligned} \]

A person can only experience cancer in age 2 only if death didnt occur in age 1. Hence, we need conditional probability. The idea is to imagine applying probability on a hypothetical newborn cohort:

3.2 Conditional on alive

ImportantMisconception

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C) &\neq \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_1}) + \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_2} \cap \text{alive in } Y_1) \\ &= 0.6 + \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_2} \mid \text{alive}) \cdot \mathbb{P}(\text{alive in } Y_1) \\ &= 0.6 + (0.5) \cdot (1 - 0.2) \\ &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

3.3 Conditional on cancer-free

Important 2: Misconception

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C) &= \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_1}) + \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_2} \cap \text{alive in } Y_1 \cap C_{\text{not in } Y_1}) \\ &= 0.6 + \mathbb{P}\left[C_{Y_2} \mid C_{\text{not in } Y_1} \cap \text{alive}\right] \cdot \mathbb{P}\underbrace{\left[C_{\text{not in } Y_1} \cap \text{alive}\right]}_{\text{alive cancer-free in } Y_2} \\ &= 0.6 + \frac{\text{cancer}}{\text{cancer} + \text{death}} \times \text{cancer in } Y_2 \text{ or death} \times \text{Survive } Y_1 \\ &\neq? 0.6 + \left[\frac{0.5}{0.5 + 0.1} \times (0.5 + 0.1)\right] \times \left[1 - (0.6 + 0.2)\right] \end{aligned} \]

3.4 Incidence Rate Approximation

But \(0.5\) is not incidence rate of cancer in \(Y_2\) because the denominator (100) in \(\frac{50}{100}\) is not population at risk. It may include people who had cancer in Age 1?

ANS: Yes, correct consideration. The AMP method acknowledges this and uses an approximation.

Suppose a population contains:

Population group People New cancer diagnoses
Never had cancer 900 90 first cancers
Previously had cancer 100 10 second cancers
Total 1,000 100

The observed cancer incidence rate is:

\[\lambda_C = \frac{100}{1000} = 0.10\]

But the actual first-cancer incidence rate is:

\[\lambda_{0C} = \frac{90}{900} = 0.10\]

Both rates equal 10%.

This works because we assumed people with and without previous cancer have the same rate of developing a new primary cancer.

Mathematically:

\[\boxed{\lambda_C = \frac{\lambda_{0C}N_0 + \lambda_{CC}N_C}{N_0 + N_C}}\]

Where:

  • \(\lambda_{0C}\) = first-cancer incidence rate
  • \(\lambda_{CC}\) = subsequent-primary cancer incidence rate
  • \(N_0\) = population without previous cancer
  • \(N_C\) = population with previous cancer

If \(\lambda_{0C} = \lambda_{CC}\), then:

\[\boxed{\lambda_C = \lambda_{0C}}\]

That is why AMP can use the overall population denominator.

But what if people with previous cancer have a higher risk?

If the 100 cancer survivors instead develop 30 new cancers:

\[\lambda_C = \frac{90 + 30}{1000} = 12\%\]

While the true first-cancer incidence remains 10%.

Therefore, AMP would overestimate the first-cancer hazard in this example.

This is a limitation of AMP, not something the calculation eliminates automatically. Sasieni et al. (2011) explain that the approximation performs well when the two groups have similar hazards or when relatively few people have previously developed cancer.

3.5 Converting Incidence Rate to Probability

In the methods above, we assumed incidence rate = probability (0,5 incidence rate = 50% chance of getting cancer). This needs corrections, arising from the fact that our crosss-sectional dataset compiles the information at a specific interval. We only see one summary slice of a completed picture. In reality, we assume that as age increases every small amount of time, risk of cancer also varies.

Also, once a person develop cancer, they are removed from the population at risk, hence probability might increase given a fixed incidence rate within the interval. For our example, a person can develop cancer at t=0.1 even if our records is compiled only at age=1.

Another concern: A car drove for 100km in one hour has rate of 100km/h. That doesn’t mean the car drove the same speed all the way, it can slow down/speed up along the way.

To account for the length of time considered and how the rate behaves over that period, under the simplifying assumption of a constant rate, developing cancer within time (t) is exponential distribution \(f(t) = re^{-rt}\). The probability of developing cancer within time (t) is \(\int_0^t re^{-ru}\,du = 1-e^{-rt}\), rather than simply \(rt\).

But why exp() though? Can be \(2^{rt}\)?

Or is it because of other reasons: memoryless property of exponential distribution? connection to poisson/survival models? For now, we think of it as a correction from discrete time to continuous.

The derivation: \[\mathbb{P}(\text{cancer}) \approx r\Delta t\] \[\mathbb{P}(\text{no cancer}) \approx 1-r\Delta t\\\] \[\left(1-\frac{r}{n}\right)^n \to e^{-r} \quad \text{as } n\to\infty\]

Time (t) (years) Approximation \(rt\) Exact probability \((1-e^{-rt}\)\)
0.01 0.50% 0.50%
0.1 5.00% 4.88%
0.5 25.00% 22.12%
1.0 50.00% 39.35%

Alternatively, \[\text{Number newly diagnosed} = \Delta S = S(t) \cdot \lambda \cdot \Delta t\] \[\ln S(t) = -\lambda t\] \[S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\] \[\mathbb{P}(\text{cancer by time t}) = 1-e^{-\lambda t}\] To account for death, we use two hazards.

Tip 3: Converting Rate to Probability, Tip 2 becomes:

Let \(\lambda_{c,j}\) be the cancer rate and \(\lambda_{d,j}\) the death rate in \(Y_j\), with \(\Lambda_j = \lambda_{c,j} + \lambda_{d,j}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C) &= \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_1}) + \mathbb{P}(C_{Y_2} \cap \text{alive in } Y_1 \cap C_{\text{not in } Y_1}) \\ &= \frac{\lambda_{c,1}}{\Lambda_1}\left(1 - e^{-\Lambda_1}\right) + \underbrace{\frac{\lambda_{c,2}}{\Lambda_2}}_{\text{cancer share}} \times \underbrace{\left(1 - e^{-\Lambda_2}\right)}_{\text{cancer or death in } Y_2} \times \underbrace{e^{-\Lambda_1}}_{\text{survive } Y_1} \\ &= \frac{0.6}{0.8}\left(1 - e^{-0.8}\right) + \frac{0.5}{0.6} \times \left(1 - e^{-0.6}\right) \times e^{-0.8} \\ &\approx 0.413 + 0.169 = 0.582 \end{aligned} \]

3.6 Generalised to AMP Method. Extending to more than 2 age groups

Let \(q_j = \dfrac{\lambda_{c,j}}{\Lambda_j}\left(1 - e^{-\Lambda_j}\right)\) be the probability of cancer in \(Y_j\), given event-free at the start of \(Y_j\).

Three age groups, written out in full: \[ \mathbb{P}(C) = q_1 + q_2\, e^{-\Lambda_1} + q_3\, e^{-\Lambda_1}\, e^{-\Lambda_2} \]

\(J\) age groups, collapsing the product of exponentials into one exponential of a sum:

TipGeneralised with extended time

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C) &= q_1 + q_2\, e^{-\Lambda_1} + q_3\, e^{-\Lambda_1} e^{-\Lambda_2} + \dots + q_J \prod_{k=1}^{J-1} e^{-\Lambda_k} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{J} q_j \prod_{k=1}^{j-1} e^{-\Lambda_k} \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{J} q_j \exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \Lambda_k\right) \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^{J} \frac{\lambda_{c,j}}{\Lambda_j}\left(1 - e^{-\Lambda_j}\right) \exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1} \Lambda_k\right) \end{aligned} \]

Note: To avoid overcounting (patient develop cancer and die), our death rate should be solely non-cancer death.
